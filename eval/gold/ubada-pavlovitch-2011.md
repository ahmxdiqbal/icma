# Pavlovitch (2011) on the ʿUbāda b. al-Ṣāmit tradition

Pavel Pavlovitch, "The ʿUbāda b. al-Ṣāmit Tradition at the Crossroads of Methodology," *Journal of Arabic and Islamic Studies* 11 (2011): 137–235. Page numbers are journal pages.

## Aim and method

Pavlovitch dates the khudhū ʿannī tradition (virgin: flogging and a year's banishment; non-virgin: flogging and stoning) twice and compares the results. First comes literary analysis of how early exegesis and law handle stoning, ranked by conceptual development (pp. 142–162). Then comes isnād-cum-matn analysis of the whole bundle (pp. 163–229). He tightens the terminology. A "key figure" is any branching point, the common link (CL) is the earliest key figure provably spreading the tradition, and an apparent CL that fails this test is a "seeming CL". Spiders prove nothing. A key figure quoted directly by a collector is presumed genuine unless shown otherwise. Collective isnāds count for little, and rijāl works are used cautiously (pp. 143–148).

## Literary analysis

Mujāhid and al-Ḍaḥḥāk explain Q 4:15–16 without stoning or extra-Quranic sources. Muqātil's unattributed dual-penalty dictum sits in a halakhic ending that looks added later. Abū ʿUbayd puts the plain version of the tradition before a version in which the Prophet shows signs of revelation. Al-Muḥāsibī prefers a stoning verse, and al-Shāfiʿī alone argues for stoning without flogging. Pavlovitch infers that the prophetic tradition appeared late in the second century AH and was soon framed as revealed (pp. 148–162).

## Non-revelation cluster

He splits the bundle by presence of a revelation preamble (p. 164). Without it, Shuʿba b. al-Ḥajjāj (d. 160/776) is the CL. His matns agree closely; Shabāba b. Sawwār is a partial common link (PCL) and Ghundar a hedged one. The many lines through ʿAlī b. al-Jaʿd form a spider, and Ibn al-Jaʿd would have needed a near-century lifespan to hear Shuʿba (pp. 164–174). Hushaym b. Bashīr is a separate CL with two PCLs (Qutayba b. Saʿīd, Yaḥyā b. Yaḥyā) and two direct collectors (pp. 174–180).

Wording ties these together. Shuʿba states the penalties as bare verbs; Hushaym turns them into noun phrases and adds the numbers (a hundred lashes, one year). Specific wording being later, Shuʿba's form belongs to the second quarter of the second century AH (p. 181). Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Qaṭṭān and Wakīʿ are later CLs who reworked Hushaym's text under new isnāds. Al-Qaṭṭān routed his through Ibn Abī ʿArūba and wrote ramyun bi-l-ḥijāra for rajm; Wakīʿ's isnād bypasses Ḥiṭṭān and ʿUbāda (pp. 181–188). These markers expose borrowing wherever matn and isnād disagree.

## Revelation cluster

The revelation versions converge loosely on Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba and Qatāda, but the evidence is single strands, doubtful key figures and copying. Ibn Manda's note on ʿUbāda, for example, reappears in Abū Nuʿaym and al-Bayhaqī. The penal maxim in every version is assembled from the Shuʿba, Hushaym and al-Qaṭṭān texts (pp. 191–208). The al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī lines are decisive. Through Jarīr b. Ḥāzim, an accepted CL, al-Ṭayālisī and ʿAbd Allāh b. Aḥmad both keep a preamble quoting Q 4:15. Yazīd b. Hārūn, also a CL, keeps a feminine-plural exclamation that only makes sense if it refers to verses. ʿAbd al-Razzāq's line through Maʿmar and Qatāda stops at al-Ḥasan (pp. 209–217).

From these Pavlovitch reconstructs al-Ḥasan's tradition: Q 4:15 was revealed, and the Prophet told his hearers to take and accept these verses. It endorses the Quranic rule and has no dual-penalty maxim (pp. 216, 227, 230). The maxim was attached later, and the seam still shows. Al-Ṭayālisī has the earliest surviving compound, ʿAbd al-Razzāq an edited one, and Abū ʿUbayd the finished form. Third-century transmitters projected the compound back onto Ibn Abī ʿArūba, Qatāda and al-Ḥasan, which explains the untidy revelation isnāds (pp. 222–226).

## Conclusions

- The dual-penalty maxim dates to Shuʿba, before about 150 AH. Whether it came from Qatāda, and whether Qatāda first joined preamble and maxim, stays unresolved (pp. 188–189, 218–221, 233).
- The earliest reconstructable layer is al-Ḥasan's Q 4:15 tradition, before 110/728.
- The compound revelation tradition formed between the last quarter of the second century and the early third (pp. 225, 233).
- Muqātil's dictum copies al-Qaṭṭān's wording, so it is a later addition (pp. 231–232).
- Juynboll made Qatāda the CL and credited al-Ḥasan with the maxim's "basic elements" (p. 163). Pavlovitch links al-Ḥasan only to the zinā question, in a scripture-based form; the maxim played no part in debate in his time (p. 234).
- Against Schacht, zinā law here starts from scripture, with the sunna secondary. Pavlovitch calls this preliminary (p. 235).
- The two methods mostly agree. Literary analysis is needed where the isnāds cannot settle the question (p. 233).

The summary table (pp. 227–229) leaves Qatāda and Ibn Abī ʿArūba with two alternative reconstructions each, and Pavlovitch admits the revelation tradition still holds ambiguities he cannot remove (p. 226).
